About 1910 to 1986

Bader, Naser, Yousef and Sager, sons of Abdulwahab

Bader Abdulwahab Al-QatamiNaser Abdulwahab Al-QatamiYousef Abdulwahab Al-QatamiSager Abdulwahab Al-Qatami

Bader was born in the Al-Qatami house in Sharq around 1910 and studied at the Ibn Rumi diwan, Ibn Sharhan's school and Al-Mubarakiya. At eleven he sailed with his father on Al-Badri and was thrown overboard by a sail rope near Jazirat Al-Sheikh on the Iranian coast; eight sailors jumped in to save him in the dark, and his mother baked a sack of flour into bread for the poor. In Zanzibar he begged to take Al-Badri to Lindi himself, and on his return the crew told his father: «Keep your pilot. Bader doesn't need him.» Off the coast of Oman he spotted the mountains of Muscat when his father did not, and his father left the ship to him.

Bader then commanded Al-Badri for five years, and Villiers met him at Mombasa in 1939 and described a tall young nakhuda in white and indigo. On the boom Al-Sulaimani during the Second World War he marked the shallow channel of the Rufiji delta with barrels and ropes and floated out a full load of mangrove poles; the marks stayed for other nakhudas and were known as «Ibn Qatami's buoys». Kuwaiti nakhudas mention him in their logbooks from 1936 to 1953. In 1954 he left the sea, piloted ships at Shuwaikh port for about fourteen years, and in the early 1980s opened a shipyard with Ustad Jassim Abdulrasool. He told Al-Hajji: «In the past we pulled the bite from the lion's mouth. Today it is put in our throats.» He died on 1 January 1986 and was buried at Sulaibikhat.

Naser called Al-Badri «the school of the sons of Abdulwahab bin Abdulaziz». At eighteen the merchant Thunayan Al-Ghanim gave him a ship to India, and when a sailor said «the nakhuda's son has come», the head of the crew answered «Quiet. This is the nakhuda.» In 1938 he sailed as navigator for Ahmed Al-Radwan, then commanded Al-Badri to Yemen and East Africa until he sold her in Aden in 1942 to Cowasjee Dinshaw. He built a new boom of about 3,000 mann and named her Al-Badri after the family ship, kept the same crew for twelve years, and later worked with his father. His sea logbook was published by the Center for Research and Kuwaiti Studies in 2002. He died on 23 March 1995.

Yousef was trained by Bader, commanded Ahmed Al-Ghanim's boom Ghazal in the early 1940s and Al-Sulaimani in the 1945 and 1946 season, then Al-Namsa in 1948 and 1950. He died young, before fifty. Sager, the youngest to command, sailed with his father on the Dau in 1938 and became nakhuda of Al-Baz in 1948, racing ahead of the fleet to Khor Mian, where his father could not believe he had arrived first. After sail ended he worked with his father and later published a guide to fishing grounds for Kuwaiti fishermen.

Sager told his own story to Al-Qabas. He was born in 1929 in Fereej Al-Mutabba in Sharq, the date read from the doorframe of the room where he was born, and went to sea at fourteen while his uncles' sons went to school and then abroad. In 1944, sailing from Calicut with two other Kuwaiti booms, he heard gunfire; his boom turned back to the Indian coast while the others went on and were struck by a Japanese submarine, and the Kuwaiti merchants at home believed the Dau had been sunk until a telegram from Muscat, forty two days later, said they were safe. Near the end of the war he sailed with his father carrying aircraft fuel in tins to the British base at Ras Riyash, and the crew put out a fire under the galley before it reached the fuel. He wrote Dalil Al-Bahr, a guide for fishermen and boat users that was printed four times, its proceeds going to the Kuwait Society for the Handicapped.
